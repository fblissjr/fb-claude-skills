Open the content up so anyone in the intended audience can understand it the first time they read it, without losing any of the substance, nuance or precision. Open up, never dumb down. This skill is a plain-language house style for American English: plain words, active voice, front-loaded structure, sentence case, and no bold or italics for emphasis.

Apply it to reports, research write-ups, guidance and any prose meant to be read. When you write a report, default to this style. When you brief a research agent, pass this skill so its report follows the same style. The structure should fit the material: a tutorial, a reference and an argument each flow differently, but each puts the reader's most important information first.

## Content design principles

- Start from the user need. Write what the reader needs to know to do or decide something, not what you want to say.
- Front-load everything. Put the most important point first, in the document, each section, each paragraph and each sentence. Use the inverted pyramid: conclusion first, then detail, then background.
- One idea per sentence. One topic per paragraph. If a sentence has more than one idea, split it.
- Be specific and concrete. Give the number, the name, the date. Cut vague abstractions ("a range of", "going forward", "in terms of").
- Cut everything that does not add meaning. Shorter is clearer. Remove duplication.

## Plain English

- Open it up, do not dumb it down. Keep all the substance, nuance and precision. Strip out only what makes it hard to read: empty jargon, long sentences, abstract nouns and tangled structure. Plain English carries complex ideas better, not worse, and even experts read faster and prefer it.
- Use the active voice. Say who does what. Write "We reviewed the data", not "The data was reviewed".
- Keep sentences short, about 15 to 20 words, never more than about 25. Keep paragraphs short.
- Replace empty bureaucratic words with plain alternatives. This targets filler, not the precise terms your audience needs:
  - use, not utilize or leverage
  - help, not facilitate or empower
  - work with, not collaborate, liaise or engage with
  - make or provide, not deliver
  - about, not in relation to or with regard to
  - so, not in order to
  - start, not commence; end, not terminate; buy, not purchase; enough, not sufficient
  - solve, fix or deal with, not tackle or combat
  - effect on, not impact on (do not use impact as a verb)
- Address the reader as "you". Write about yourself or the organization as "we". Use "they", "them" and "their" rather than gendered pronouns. Write "disabled people", not "the disabled".
- Contractions are fine for a warmer tone (we'll, you'll), but avoid negative contractions (write "cannot", not "can't") and avoid "should've", "could've", "would've".

## Match the audience and keep real terminology

Plain English means clear, not a smaller vocabulary. Do not confuse cutting jargon with cutting precision.

- Use the precise, established term when it carries real meaning for the reader. Write for the actual audience. For experts, keep the field's real terminology and do not soften an exact term into vague paraphrase.
- The test is meaning, not difficulty. Use a term when it is precise and in current use in the field. Cut it when it is vague, ambiguous, or not actually established. A word that sounds technical but that no practitioner uses has not earned its place.
- Example: for a frontier-AI audience, terms like KV cache, speculative decoding, logits and context window are exact and expected; use them plainly. Invented or fuzzy coinages that no one in the field uses do not belong, however technical they sound.
- Define a term only when part of your audience will not know it, and then define it once, in passing. Do not define terms your audience uses every day.

## Cut filler and the machine-generated register

Large language models reach for a recognizable set of words and frames that a person with something to say would not. When this style is invoked, none of them belong in the output. The lists below are representative, not exhaustive. The governing test: would a domain expert, writing plainly, choose this phrase, or is it a reflex the model reaches for to sound like writing? If it is the reflex, cut it, along with anything you could delete without losing meaning.

- Faux-profundity words standing in for a precise one: load-bearing, heavy lifting, delve, dive into, deep dive, unpack, navigate, landscape, ecosystem, realm, tapestry, testament to, underscore, boast, seamless, robust, elevate, empower, unlock, harness, foster, drive, crucial, pivotal, vital, game-changer, supercharge, ring-fence, hub, portal.
- Formulaic frames: "it is not X, it is Y" and "not just X, but Y" as a crutch rather than a real distinction; "whether you are X or Y"; "in the world of X"; "at its core"; a rule-of-three list where the third item is padding.
- False candor and self-narration: "the honest truth", "let us be honest", "to be clear", "to be fair", "it is worth noting", "it is important to note", "importantly", "notably".
- Empty connective filler: "that said", "at the end of the day", "when it comes to", "going forward", "needless to say", "ultimately", "essentially", "fundamentally".
- Reflex openers and closers: "in today's fast-paced world", "let us dive in", "buckle up", and "in conclusion" or "in summary" used as a reflex rather than a genuine recap.
- Em-dash overuse. Leaning on the em dash (—) is one of the clearest tells. Prefer a period, a comma, or a single hyphen, or restructure the sentence. A single hyphen in a compound (real-time, well-scoped, end-to-end) is always fine.

## American English conventions

Use American forms throughout.

- Spelling: use American spelling everywhere: organize (not organise), color, behavior, center, defense, canceled, traveled, catalog, analyze, license (noun and verb), program. Prefer American word choices: toward (not towards), while (not whilst), among (not amongst).
- Quotation marks: use double quotation marks as the default. Use single quotation marks only for a quote inside a quote.
- Punctuation and quotes: put periods and commas inside the closing quotation mark, American style. She called it "front-loading," and it worked. Colons, semicolons, question marks and exclamation points go inside only when they belong to the quoted material.
- Serial comma: use the serial (Oxford) comma before the final "and" or "or" in a list of three or more, as in "plain English, active voice, and sentence case". It removes ambiguity.
- Dates: write the month first, as in January 5, 2026, or "January 2026" for a month and year. For all-numeric dates use the ISO form (2026-01-05) to avoid ambiguity; do not write 01/05/2026.
- Numbers: use American number formatting, a comma for thousands (1,000) and a period for the decimal point (3.14).

## Formatting

- Do not use bold or italics for emphasis. Plain words and good structure carry the meaning. Bold is only acceptable to name a literal interface element in an instruction, for example: select Save. Use double quotation marks for the titles of programs or documents, not italics.
- Use sentence case everywhere: headings, titles, table headers, the lot. Capitalize only proper nouns.
- Headings: front-load them, keep them under about 65 characters, make them unique and descriptive. No period, dash, slash or question mark. Use them to let people skim.
- Bullet points: introduce the list with a lead-in line that ends in a colon. Start each bullet lowercase. Keep each to one idea. No "and"/"or" after each item, no semicolons, no period after the last bullet (unless a bullet is itself a full sentence).
- Numbered steps: use a numbered list only for a sequence the reader follows in order. Steps are full sentences and end with a period. No lead-in colon needed.
- Links: use descriptive link text that says where the link goes, front-loading the key words. Never write "click here" or "read more". The link text should make sense out of context.
- Do not use Latin abbreviations. Write "for example" not "e.g.", "that is" not "i.e.", "and so on" or "such as" not "etc." They confuse screen readers and some readers.
- Ampersands: write "and", not "&" (except in a registered name or logo).
- Do not use FAQs. If you have answered the user need in the content, you do not need them. Do not use exclamation marks. Do not use ALL CAPS for emphasis.

## Before you finish: self-check

- Is the single most important thing first?
- Could the intended reader understand every sentence on first read?
- Does every technical term carry real, current meaning for this audience, with nothing vague or invented?
- Is every sentence active, short and one idea?
- Did you run a final pass for the machine-generated register (load-bearing, delve, "it is not X, it is Y", em-dash overuse) and cut every instance?
- Have you removed all bold/italic emphasis, empty jargon, Latin abbreviations and marketing language?
- Is the spelling American and the punctuation American (double quotes, periods and commas inside, serial comma)?
- Is everything in sentence case, with descriptive headings and links?
- Could you cut any more words without losing meaning? If yes, cut them.

## Note on this skill's own scope

The "no bold" and formatting rules apply to the prose you produce (reports, guidance, summaries). Code, data tables and direct quotations keep their own conventions. Markdown headings and lists are fine; they are structure, not emphasis.
